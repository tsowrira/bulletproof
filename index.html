// How to Run the Week — Order & When to Rest

Four sessions, skill every day: Pull → Legs A → (cardio or rest) → Push → Legs B. Nothing hard trains two days running — legs land Tue + Fri, uppers Mon + Thu, Wednesday breathes. Every session opens with one ~8-min Skill Block while your brain is fresh: skill is motor learning, and motor learning wants frequent short doses, not one hoarded Friday. Four days is the plan — miss one and the week still works; never make one up by stacking.

The 2-Set Rule — every lift, two working sets: Set 1 solid at RIR 2 · Set 2 AMRAP — as many clean reps as you have, stopping at form break, any pain, or one rep left in the tank. Log the number; next session's game is beating it. Two hard sets near failure capture most of the stimulus of longer schemes (Iversen 2021 time-efficient training; 2023 proximity-to-failure meta) at a fraction of the time and joint cost — and nothing on this page repeats across the week, so every session is its own menu. Spine/knee exceptions: goblet squat, BSS, step-up, and the cable pull-through run fixed reps, no AMRAP — the disc doesn't do failure; the side-bend resist runs on time, not reps. Machines are where AMRAP lives; when set 2 hits ~15 clean, go up one pin and rebuild. PT-gated loads stay gated no matter how easy they feel.

Within-session order + fatigue triage

Order within a session: knee-quieting isometric (leg days) → skill while your brain is fresh → main compound → accessories → isolation → core/anti-rotation last. Never grind skill or a loaded hinge once you're cooked — that's when hypermobile form slips.

Tired? Read it, don't push through: flat/heavy → keep the session, drop a set or two, hold the load. Run-down (bad sleep, RPE up two sessions, achy) → swap the hard day for skill + a walk or StairMaster, or take the rest day early. Wiped, or any new leg signal → full rest day, and pull the deload forward a week. When the week compresses, protect Legs A & B first; skill blocks are cheap enough to keep — sacrifice cardio first. A planned rest beats a forced one. Full green/yellow/red guide in Mesocycle.

⚠ Form Rules — Non-Negotiable · 2-Set-Rule exception: NO AMRAP here

(1) Neutral spine, ribs stacked over pelvis — the goblet hold in front of the chest is what keeps the torso upright and the flexion moment small; the instant the low back would round (butt wink), the rep is over and depth comes up. (2) Depth is earned by control — at/above parallel while the right knee is noisy; never bounce out of the bottom. (3) Knees track over toes, both sides — watch the left for inward drift. Heels stay planted the whole rep.

Setup & Execution
1. Setup: hold one 15 lb dumbbell vertically at the sternum, elbows down (not flared), feet shoulder-width, toes slightly out.
2. Brace: gentle 360° abdominal tension, set your natural lordosis, eyes forward.
3. Descend: 3-count — hips back and down together, chest proud, weight mid-foot. Stop at parallel or the first hint of pelvis tuck, whichever comes first.
4. Pause 1 count at the bottom — dead still, no bounce.
5. Drive: push the floor away through the whole foot, squeeze glutes at the top — no leaning back, no rib flare, knees never lock.
🎯 Coaching Cue
"Elbows to thighs, chest proud." The dumbbell is a counterweight — let it keep you honest and upright. Any session mechanics feel off: one bodyweight set first, broomstick check (head, mid-back, sacrum touching = neutral).

McGill (2007, 2015) established neutral-spine pattern control as the prerequisite for re-loading after disc injury — and the goblet squat is the canonical pattern-teacher: the anterior load forces an upright torso, which keeps the lumbar flexion moment small exactly where Adams & Dolan's annular work says combined bending + compression does the damage. An upright 15 lb goblet squat carries a fraction of the disc cost of the same load held low in a hinge.

Why weights beat bands now (the clinic's own rationale): progressive external load drives muscle strength and bone-mineral adaptation — bone responds to load magnitude and novelty, which band tension can't progress cleanly. LIFTMOR-style trials showed supervised progressive loading is both effective and safe for bone in small-framed women. The gate is the dose, not the modality: 15 lb, clinic-verified, climbing only on re-clearance.

— McGill (2007, 2015); Adams & Dolan; Watson et al. (LIFTMOR, 2018) on loading + bone; Hayden et al. (2021) Cochrane post-disc exercise review

Setup & Execution
Kneel on the Nordic pad. Ankles secured by the machine pulley (or anchored under a heavy bench). Body is one rigid line — head, hips, knees stacked. Tip forward from the knees over 5 full seconds — knees extend, hips stay locked in line with the body (no hip bend). The torso travels toward the floor as a single unit. 🎯 Coaching Cue

Petersen's landmark RCT in elite Danish soccer players cut hamstring injury rate by ~60% (new injuries) and ~85% (recurrence) over a season. Subsequent meta-analyses (van Dyk 2019; Al Attar) pooled ~51% across heterogeneous protocols and confirmed the mechanism: hamstring fascicle length increases under eccentric overload, shifting the force-length curve into a more protective range.

With hypermobile knees, the eccentric Nordic also creates co-contraction signal across the posterior knee that supports the joint. The "stop 15° before extension" rule keeps load on muscle rather than joint capsule, which matters disproportionately for hypermobile athletes.

— Petersen et al. (2011); van Dyk et al. (2019); Mendiguchia et al.; Al Attar et al.

// Stay Hooked — Variety Without Losing Progress

VARIETY & SKILL LADDERS

Boredom kills adherence, and you love calisthenics — so the program is built as a fixed protective core + rotatable accessories + skill you level up. Variety lives in the accessories and the ladders; the core lifts you actually track stay put.

🔒 Fixed vs 🔁 Rotatable

Fixed (keep ~4–6 weeks): the first 1–2 exercises each day — the ones you progressively overload and log — plus the protective core (goblet squat, glute-med, Nordic, side plank, bird-dog, face pull). These are insurance, not variety slots.

Rotatable (swap every 3–4 weeks): the accessory slots. Same target, different stimulus — fresh without resetting progress. A varied-accessory group matched a fixed group on strength + size but scored higher on motivation (Rafsanjani 2019).

🎮 Skill Ladders — the calisthenics game (advance when you own a level)

Handstand: wall hold (back-to-wall) → chest-to-wall hold → chest-to-wall shoulder taps → toe-pulls off the wall → 1–2s free balance → 5s → 10s → shapes. Wall is always the catch; press through active shoulders, never hang on locked joints.

Pull-up (you own ~6 strict): strict 4×4–5 → +2.5–5 lb weighted → archer → typewriter → assisted one-arm. Active grip, soft elbow at the bottom — never a passive or locked-out hang.

Front lever — the far-future long-lever skill (the legal dragon-flag itch): scap pulls (you're here — they live in the Bar block) → tuck hold (not yet, and that's the honest answer) → advanced tuck → single-leg → straddle. When tuck day comes: hard posterior pelvic tilt the whole time, end the rep the instant the low back sags. Soft elbows, scaps packed.

Pistol: box sit (tall) → lower box → step-platform heel-tap (fingertips on a rung for balance only) → no-contact off the step. Off a step, never a wall; cap at parallel — do not chase full depth (hypermobile knees lose structural support past parallel and it spikes kneecap load).

Rule: stay on a level until it's clean for 3 reps/holds across 2 sessions, then unlock the next. Progress-gated, not calendar-gated — that's the dopamine.

⚖️ Two rules so variety never costs you progress
  • Never vary AND add load in the same week — when you swap an exercise, keep the weight conservative for week 1 while the motor pattern re-grooves.
  • Log the fixed lifts every session. Accessory variety is noise; your tracked compounds are the signal. As long as those climb, rotate the rest freely.
Daily Low-Load
// Multifidus + Glute Med + Decompression · Every Day · ~8 minutes

Three movements every day, low-load, high-frequency. The rest of the daily menu (side-lying multifidus lift, hip CARs, chin nods, SLR nerve glide) rotates 3×/week to avoid skip-fatigue.

⏰ When to Do These — Morning, Before the Day Pulls You

Default slot is first thing in the morning, before coffee or scrolling, while listening to a podcast or just waking up. Why morning:

  • Multifidus benefits from frequency, not intensity — earlier in the day means it actually happens. Evening "I'll do it after dinner" routines drop off fast (compliance research is brutal on this).
  • Bird-dog primes hip-to-shoulder connection for the rest of the day — useful even on non-training days.
  • Decompression hang reverses overnight disc-water-loss (your discs hydrate flat, lose hydration upright; ~15s of hang in the morning is the cheapest version of "morning back routine").

Sequence (~8 min total): wake up → bathroom → bird-dog on the floor (2 min) → clamshell (1 min) → walk to the Swedish ladder for decompression hang (15s × 2, with 30s walk between) → done. Second hang any time before bed.

If the morning doesn't happen: any other slot still beats skipping. The hierarchy is "done sometime" > "done in the right slot" > "done perfectly."

Mild — annoying but no new leg signals

Sharper — interrupting sleep or daily stuff

Pause training. Daily walking + decompression hang only. If it's still loud past 48 hours or escalating, ping PT. The "just push through" instinct is the wrong one — three quiet days protects the next three months.

🧠 HOW YOUR BACK ACTUALLY WORKS

A history of disc trouble means the disc has been mechanically vulnerable. It does not mean your back is fragile in the way the catastrophic injury feels suggest. Three things are simultaneously true and worth holding together:

  • Discs heal. The annular fibers form scar tissue over 6–12 months of healing; healed disc tissue is not the same as never-injured disc tissue, but it is meaningfully resilient. Adams & Roughley's nutrition work documents the timeline.
  • Multifidus re-recruits. Post-disc multifidus atrophy is real and persistent — but daily, low-load, high-frequency motor-control work (bird-dog, dead bug) demonstrably restores recruitment over weeks to months (Hodges & Richardson; Hides et al.).
  • Pain ≠ damage in the chronic phase. By 6+ months into recovery, pain signal often outlives tissue threat. Louw et al.'s meta-analyses on pain neuroscience education show medium-to-large effects on fear-of-load and catastrophizing — relevant because fear-of-load is its own training limiter.

Hold this alongside the Red Flag list. The framework is "trust the trajectory + respect the signals" — both at once, neither alone.

📊 Monthly Test Battery — Track What the Program Is Actually Doing

First Saturday of every month, ~20 minutes. Log in the same note. No interpretation needed mid-test — write the numbers, look at the trend at the 3-month mark. Three months of data is when patterns become visible. Don't act on a single month's reading.

  1. McGill side-plank endurance, L vs R. Hold to failure, both sides. Target: L = R within 5%, both > 60s. R/L difference > 15% is the flag.
  2. McGill back-extensor endurance (Biering-Sørensen). Hold prone-extension over a bench/table to failure. Target: > 90s. Below 60s is the persistent-LBP flag in McGill's data.
  3. Single-leg balance, eyes closed. 30s target each leg — a general proprioception + ankle check, not a strength test. A persistent >5s side gap is worth noting; small differences are normal.
  4. Y-Balance Test, lower quarter (anterior reach). Stand on one leg, reach the other foot forward, mark distance. Anterior reach asymmetry > 4 cm is the rehab-literature flag (Plisky; Smith).
  5. Single-leg sit-to-stand × 5. From a chair, one leg, no hand assist. L < R by > 20% = quad/glute deficit on that side.
  6. Joint-position-replication test, knee. Sit on a table edge, eyes closed. Move knee to a target angle, return, repeat. Off by > 10° = proprioceptive flag (Russek 2024).

Phase 1 — Heading Into It (~7 days before)

Standard program runs. Two small shifts: drop new 1RM testing and aggressive eccentric overload (hold the prior 4 weeks' loads). Spend the runway on technique polish, not PRs. Sleep + protein adequacy are the highest-leverage levers heading in.

Phase 2 — Quietest Window (~10–14 days)

The window where heavy bracing, inversion, and high-impact work are temporarily off the table. Background reason: Valsalva drives intra-abdominal pressure to 100–200 mmHg (Hackett & Chow 2013), and when there's any reason to keep that down, the lifts that demand it have to come out.

OUT: heavy hip thrust · BSS · loaded hinge · Nordic · pull-ups + dead hangs (Valsalva + traction) · wall handstand (inversion) · running · jumping · plyometrics · HIIT · spin bike · flip turns · machine ab/adduction at full ROM (pelvic rotation) · twisting / lateral-flexion under load.

KEEP: walking 30–45 min/day. Continuous-lap swim with open turns only. Daily Low-Load. Light upper-body cable work (face pull, prone Y, light row) at ≤50% RPE, no breath-holding. Half-kneeling / supported variants only. Moderate exercise during these windows is well-supported by the trials available (e.g. ASRM 2024 RCT) — the framing is "drop intensity, keep moving."

Phase 3 — First 5 Days After the Peak

Day 0–2: walking only, 10–20 min, flat ground. Nothing over 5 lb. Day 3–5: extend walking to 30 min, reintroduce supine Daily Low-Load (bird-dog + dead bug only — skip side plank, lateral compression is the limiter). Cap at 10 lb, no breath-holding, no inversions, no swimming if there's any infection-window consideration.

Phase 4 — Ramp Back (2–6 weeks)

Rule of thumb: resume strenuous training only when the body has clearly settled back to its baseline (e.g. cycle resumed as normal, no lingering soreness, sleep back to baseline).

Wk 2: full Daily Low-Load. Bodyweight squats, glute bridges, light cable row, knee push-ups. No Valsalva, no eccentric-emphasis. RPE cap 6.

Wk 3: reintroduce hinge teacher, goblet squat at prior load −30%, hip thrust at prior load −30%. Swim resumes (open turns). RPE cap 7.

Wk 4+: reintroduce Nordic (top half first), pull-up negatives at 5s eccentric (not the prior 8s), BSS bodyweight → 5 lb → 10 lb. Inversions last. Full prior loads by week 5–6 if no flare.

Supplement overlay for the window

Creatine: stay on at 5 g/day — well-tolerated and useful for cognitive + recovery markers across phases. (Only change if a clinician specifically suggests it for a unique context.)

CoQ10 ubiquinol: keep on at 200–400 mg continuously. Mitochondrial support that doesn't need to come off for any phase.

Vitamin D: keep at 2000–4000 IU; no phase-specific bolus.

NAC (optional): 1.8 g/day from start to end of the active window has been associated with better recovery markers in some clinical contexts (Cheraghi 2016, 2022). Clinician-gated, not a default.

Iron: recheck serum ferritin periodically — vegetarian athletes are prone to silent depletion, and any hormonal, dietary, or training change can shift it.

When in doubt in Phase 2 or 3, the default answer is "walk + Daily Low-Load only" — the always-safe floor.

🦵 RECOVERY TOOLS — NormaTec + Soft Tissue

NormaTec boots are a recovery tool, not a warmup — pre-training they down-regulate you, the opposite of what you want. Honest evidence: a small-to-moderate effect on soreness (peaks ~48h) and a "lighter legs" feeling, but no boost to strength return or hypertrophy. Worth it for comfort that keeps you training, not for gains.

  • After Legs A / Legs B: 20–30 min, ~60–80 mmHg, post-session or that evening (finish ~60 min before bed).
  • Rest / heavy-leg days: 20 min, 40–60 mmHg, evening — great when you skipped the pool and legs feel cumulative.
  • Quiet / clinician-flagged windows: totally fine — no spinal or joint load, and hypermobility-safe (circumferential pressure, no joint torque).
  • Skip if: any lower-leg DVT concern, skin infection, or an open/unhealed site. Start lower-pressure and build.

Massage gun + triangular lacrosse ball live at the front of the leg-day warmups (tone relief on tight spots) — see those days. Fine post-session for comfort too. Same hypermobile rule everywhere: relieve guarded tissue, never chase range.

🍽️ Training When Intake / Appetite Is Low

Some weeks intake runs low — travel, low appetite, a deliberate cut, a clinician-managed phase. The rule flips from "build" to "retain": the program becomes anti-catabolic insurance, not a growth block.

  • Hold the load, cut volume if needed — keep working weights; drop a set before you drop intensity. The heavy-ish stimulus is what tells the body to keep the muscle.
  • Don't fall below ~10 hard sets/week on priority muscles (lats, delts, glutes, quads, hams) — the lean-mass-retention floor in a deficit (Roth 2022/2023).
  • Protein first — ≥1.6 g/kg bodyweight even when appetite's low (liquid protein counts — see Fuel). Biggest lean-mass lever in a deficit.
  • Trim eccentric volume — Nordics and long pull-up negatives cost the most recovery; cap them when under-fuelled.
  • Creatine stays on, daily — a lean-mass/strength ally exactly here.
  • Read the signals — sharp strength/bodyweight drops or a wandering cycle mean too little fuel: raise intake or consolidate to only the top-priority sets (don't just grind more).
🌙 SLEEP & ENERGY AVAILABILITY
Sleep — single biggest recovery lever

Target 7.5–9h/night. Dark room, no screens 30 min pre-sleep. Track weekly average, not nightly variance. Dattilo 2011 + Knowles 2018: sleep is the single largest hypertrophy + tissue-healing variable. More important than any supplement.

Energy availability — eat enough for the work

1500 kcal is the low edge for a small-framed active female. Cycle regularity is a downstream signal — if cycle goes irregular during a high-volume training block, energy availability is too low. Add 200–300 kcal/day and recheck in 4–6 weeks (Mountjoy et al. on RED-S).

// Periodization · One Clock for All Four Days

MESOCYCLE & DELOAD

A 6-week block under the 2-Set Rule: sets stay at two everywhere — what climbs across the block is effort (how deep set 2's AMRAP goes) and tempo, then one deload week at easy effort. Same clock across all four training days; skill blocks ride along untouched (they're practice, not volume). Per-tissue rules below override the calendar when they conflict.

⚙ Per-Tissue Progression Speeds — overrides the calendar

Muscle adapts in ~3 weeks; tendon takes 8+ (Bohm 2015 meta; Magnusson 2008). Lifts where the limiter is tendon get a 2-mesocycle progression cadence (12 weeks per load step) instead of 6.

  • Tendon-limited (12-wk per step): Nordic curl, pull-up 6s negatives, dead hang time. Load or assist changes happen at wk 12 (start of mesocycle 3), not wk 4 or 8 within a mesocycle.
  • Muscle-limited (6-wk per step): cable rows, kneeling pulldown, hip thrust load, BSS load (PT gate-permitting), vest push-up plus, Pallof.
  • Motor-control / skill (continuous): handstand wall holds, shoulder taps, dead bug, bird-dog, balance work. No deload — just don't add holds during deload week. These are nervous-system, not tissue-stress.

Practical: when a Wk 4 / Wk 8 / Wk 12 step appears on a tendon-limited card, read it as "start of mesocycle 1 / 2 / 3," not as calendar weeks. The Nordic 7.5 lb → 5 lb → 2.5 lb path runs across 18 weeks of training, not 12.

📡 Autoregulation — Performance Signals Beat Wearable Scores

Wearable readiness scores (HRV, RHR) are useful as long-trend personal baselines but unreliable as day-to-day fatigue measures — too many inputs shift them (sleep, cycle phase, daily routines, hydration, anything you ingested). Use the performance + symptom signals below as the primary read.

Green-light to push set 2 deeper / chase an AMRAP PR:

  • Top-set RIR feels at-or-below the planned RIR (your subjective sense of "1 left" matches what you actually finished)
  • No new back signals — morning AM stiffness ≤ 5 min
  • Sleep weekly average ≥ 7.5 h
  • Sessions completing in planned time without rest-period inflation

Yellow — match last week's numbers, no PR attempts:

  • Same load feels 0.5–1 RPE harder for 2 consecutive sessions (Helms; Chiang 2025 on RPE drift as early fatigue signal)
  • Eccentric tempo cheating — you're rushing the 6-count on pull-up negatives without meaning to
  • Sleep weekly avg dipped below 7 h
  • Cycle irregularity in a non-cycle-monitored month → fuel signal (Mountjoy RED-S)

Red — pull the deload forward by a week:

  • Any new leg signal (per Signs to Check In About list in Framework)
  • Two consecutive sessions where you couldn't finish the planned top set at planned RIR
  • Morning back stiffness extending past 15 min for 3+ consecutive days
  • Goblet squat feels different at the same load — even subtly

🔀 Flare Week vs Scheduled Deload — Do They Double Up?

A flare-driven downshift counts as that mesocycle's deload. Resume the next block at MEV (wk 1 settings), not at wk 5 where you left off. Two consecutive downshifts in a 6-week window overcompensates and breaks tendon adaptation continuity.

Exception: if the flare-deload happens in wk 1 or wk 2, run a normal 4-week ramp from wk 3 and keep the scheduled wk-6 deload — the flare was too early in the block to count as adequate recovery.

Tracking: mark the calendar week the flare downshift occurred. If a scheduled deload would fall within ±1 week of a flare deload, skip the scheduled one. Daily Low-Load auto-de-escalates per Framework — the rule here is just to not double-deload accidentally.

↺ Quieter-Window Interaction with the Mesocycle

If you're in any temporary-restriction window (see Framework · Temporary-Restriction Overlay for the menu), here's how it interacts with the 6-week clock:

The full window counts as one deload of the mesocycle clock — whether the window is one week or four. Don't add a scheduled deload on top.

Resume at MEV (wk 1) of the next block on the first full training week post-clearance. Do not try to "catch up" — the body just had a forced taper, treat it like one.

Re-test items pause during the window; pick them up at the next regularly-scheduled deload week.

📋 Worked Example — One Block, Two Lifts

Bulgarian split squat and goblet squat across one 6-week block. PT gates take priority over calendar steps.

Bulgarian Split Squat (muscle-limited — 6-wk cadence)

  • Wk 1: 7.5 lb × 2, 2×8 each side, RIR 3, tempo 3·2·2
  • Wk 2: 2×8, second set pushed toward 9–10 clean (RIR 2)
  • Wk 3: 2×9–10, 2s bottom hold on set 1
  • Wk 4: hold reps, eccentric slows to 4s
  • Wk 5 · peak: both sets at 10 clean, RIR 1–2 — or if PT re-clearance happened, step to 10 lb × 2 for 2×6 and rebuild
  • Wk 6 · deload: 2×8 easy, RIR 4–5. Retest single-leg balance EC at the final session.

Goblet Squat (disc-limited — same 15 lb all block, always 2 fixed sets, never AMRAP)

  • Wk 1: 15 lb, 2×10, tempo 3·1·2 — bed the pattern in, log knee + back response
  • Wk 2: 2×10 with a 1–2s pause at the bottom (control the deep range)
  • Wk 3: 2×11–12, pause kept
  • Wk 4: tempo 4·1·2 (slower eccentric) — full quality stimulus
  • Wk 5 · peak: 2×12, 4·2·2 with the bottom pause
  • Wk 6 · deload: 2×8, tempo 3·1·2, no pause. Retest squat RPE at the final session.
  • Mesocycle 2 (wks 7–12): same 15 lb. Add density (rest 90→75s), not load. Disc adaptation is biological (Adams & Roughley).
  • Load step: 20 lb only behind PT re-clearance — then reset to 2×10 and rebuild.

The contrast is the point. The BSS climbs in reps and effort inside the block. The goblet squat climbs in tempo and density only — the load number moves on clinic re-clearance, because the disc is the binding constraint and it doesn't care what week the calendar says.

// Non-Negotiable Rules

HYPERMOBILITY PROTOCOL

Joint hypermobility means the nervous system has learned to rely on passive structures — ligaments and joint capsules — instead of muscles. Every rule below actively retrains that pattern toward active stabilization.

Never Lock Out

Stop 10–15° before full extension — elbows, knees, hips. The click is capsular loading, not muscular control. That distinction matters for everything.

Warmup Every Session

Hypermobile joints need neural activation before load. This is where hypermobile athletes get hurt. No exceptions.

Proprioception Work

Eyes-closed balance + random perturbation retrain position sense. The nuance that matters for you: active position sense is good, passive end-range sense is the blind spot — stay actively engaged, never relax into end range.

Isometrics Are Medicine

Every hold in this program teaches muscles to fire without passive end-range joint reliance. The core retraining goal.

No Passive Stretching

No yin yoga. No passive splits. No hanging end-range. More passive range without control = more instability.

Tendons Take 8+ Weeks

Muscles adapt in 2–3 weeks. Tendons 8+. When something feels easy, wait 4 more weeks before adding load.

Closed-Chain Mid-Range

All hypertrophy progressions: closed-chain, mid-range, isometric → eccentric → concentric. Co-contraction cues, not feeling-the-stretch cues.

HRV in Trends Only

Use HRV / RHR as own-baseline trends, not absolute readiness scores. Many inputs shift baselines — sleep, cycle phase, hydration, anything pharmacologic you've added or changed. Day-to-day variance is noise; multi-week drift with no obvious cause is the signal worth acting on.

// Approach & Evidence — Why Each Choice

Five principles — why the program is built this way

Built around real joint limits: a back that won't tolerate loaded flexion, joints that need active control over passive range, and knees that flare with the wrong loading — while still progressing without setbacks. Machines, free weights, cables, and bodyweight are each chosen per movement for whichever is safest and most effective — not by dogma. Every principle below is backed by published rehab and exercise-science literature.

01 · Spine-Sparing
Hinge, don't flex under load

Cumulative lumbar flexion under load is the dominant mechanism for annular damage. Replace flexion-bias movements (deadlift bottom, hollow body, loaded RDL) with an upright anterior-loaded squat pattern (goblet), dowel-grooved hinge work, anti-rotation (Pallof), and dead bug.

McGill (2002, 2007, 2015); Adams & Dolan; Callaghan & McGill
02 · Multifidus First
Daily, low-load, high-frequency segmental control

Multifidus atrophies after disc injury and does not automatically rebound. Retraining requires daily, low-load, high-frequency motor-control work — not heavy stabilization. Bird-dog with holds and prone unilateral isometrics are the canonical rebuilders.

Hodges & Richardson (1996, 1999); Hides et al. (1996, 2008)
03 · Hypermobility Protocol
Closed-chain, mid-range, isometric → eccentric → concentric

Hypermobile joints lean on ligaments and capsules when active control fails. Inverted training rules: no end-range under load, prioritize co-contraction, build isometric → eccentric → concentric. Cables and machines carry the smooth-tension work; free weights stay mid-range and clinic-dosed.

Russek (1999); Simmonds & Keer (2007); Engelbert et al.; Mottram
04 · Effort Over Volume
Two hard sets per lift — the second an AMRAP you try to beat

Two working sets taken close to failure capture the large majority of the hypertrophy stimulus of longer set schemes, at a fraction of the session time and joint cost — the right trade for slow-adapting tendons and a load-sensitive spine. Hypertrophy responds across 5–30 reps near failure; true failure isn't required (the AMRAP stops at form break or one rep in reserve). Machines carry the AMRAP work — a stack can't be dropped on you, and load climbs in clean increments at low spinal cost. Spine-loaded lifts are the exception: fixed reps, never AMRAP.

Iversen et al. (2021) time-efficient training; Schoenfeld (2017, 2019); proximity-to-failure meta (2023); Israetel landmarks
05 · Asymmetry & Targeting
Point the extra volume where the deficit actually is now — the right side

The weaker (right) side gets the 2-1 method: two unilateral sets right for every one left, until symmetry returns. Both knees get pain-free quad loading (full-arc leg extension with a hard stop at straight, wall-sit isometrics) and glute-med work — which is knee medicine, beating knee-only work for front-of-knee pain. Volume points to the side with the live deficit, not to old history.

Contreras 2-1 method; Halabi et al. (2025) hip+knee for PFP; Rio (2015) isometric analgesia

Hodges & Richardson — Multifidus dysfunction after disc injury; motor-control retraining as the foundation of post-injury rehab. Anchors the Daily Low-Load priority.
Hides, Richardson & Jull — Multifidus does not spontaneously recover after first-episode LBP; targeted retraining required. Why bird-dog goes daily, not weekly.
Stuart McGill — Spine-sparing principles, Big 3 (curl-up · side plank · bird-dog), endurance > strength for chronic spine. Anchors the Big 3 finisher and the no-loaded-flexion rule.
Adams & Dolan; Callaghan & McGill — Annular fiber failure under bending + compression at far lower loads than pure compression. Reason for permanent shelving of conventional deadlift and the SL-RDL deferral.
Schoenfeld et al. (2017, 2019) — Volume-hypertrophy curve; rep-range agnostic with proximity to failure. Justifies the volume cuts (lat raise 4×15→3×12, etc.) without strength penalty.
Petersen et al. (2011); van Dyk et al. (2019); Al Attar et al. — Petersen's RCT: ~60% reduction in new hamstring injuries and ~85% in recurrences with eccentric Nordic protocol. Meta-pooled effect across heterogeneous trials lands closer to ~51% (van Dyk, Al Attar). Why Nordic stays in the program despite its difficulty.
Cools et al. (2007); Reinold & Wilk (2007) — Prone elevation ~120–145° thumbs-up favorably loads the lower trap with low upper-trap cost. Selection of the prone Y over generic "Y-T-W" combos.
Distefano et al. (2009); Boren et al. (2011); Selkowitz et al. (2013) — Side-lying hip abduction and side-plank variants rank among the highest glute-med EMG exercises; clamshells are markedly lower. Why side-lying abduction is the driver and the clamshell is a pattern groomer. Exact MVIC values and clamshell hip angles vary by study — treated as a ranking, not precise numbers.
Adams & Roughley; Belavy et al. (2017, on running) — Disc tissue adapts to mechanical load; low-magnitude axial unloading and intermittent dynamic loading (walking, running) maintain hydration. Sustained sitting is the actual disc-degenerator. Anchors the daily decompression hang and walking emphasis over reverse hypers. Note: Belavy's specific paper is on running, not hanging — the hanging extension is consistent with the broader mechanism but is inference.
Russek (1999); Simmonds & Keer (2007); Engelbert; Scheper — Hypermobility-specific training: closed-chain, mid-range, isometric → eccentric → concentric, no end-range under load. Anchors all the depth caps and "never lock out" rules.
Snyder-Mackler; Buckthorpe et al.; Risberg et al. — Persistent quadriceps (especially VMO) deficits are common after a knee injury without targeted work. Anchors the step-up + knee-tracking warmup.
Lavagnino, Jenkyn (vertical-shin / decline-board work) — Vertical-shin patellofemoral-friendly quad loading reduces patellar tendon strain vs. forward-knee squat. Why the wall-sit is the knee-quieting primer, and why quad depth stays capped while the knee is noisy.
Ekstrand; Bahr & Krosshaug; Mendiguchia — Sports injury epidemiology; eccentric overload for tendon resilience. Background for the slow-eccentric emphasis throughout.
Maeo et al. (2021) — Hamstring hypertrophy is greater with the hip flexed (lengthened) in the leg curl; gastrocnemius grows with the knee straight (standing > seated calf raise). Why the leg curl targets length and the calf raise is done standing.
Knee-extensor PFP systematic review (2025); Witvrouw OKC-vs-CKC 5-yr RCT — Leg extension is not contraindicated in patellofemoral pain; ROM capping is a titration tool, not a permanent rule. Anchors the leg-extension reintroduction; the 90→45° cap served its purpose and was lifted by the clinic July 2026 — the hyperextension stop remains.
Rio et al. (2015) — Heavy isometric holds (5 × 45s) gave ~45 min of analgesia in patellar tendinopathy; extension to patellofemoral pain is inferred (low-risk). Why the isometric leads the knee day.
Halabi et al. (2025) — Hip + knee strengthening beats knee-only for patellofemoral pain (large pooled effect). Why glute-med work is treated as knee medicine.
Contreras (2-1 method); Plotkin et al. (2023) — Extra unilateral volume on the weak side restores symmetry; hip thrust ≈ squat for glute-max hypertrophy. Anchors the right-glute 2-1 dosing and the hip-thrust selection.
Resistance-training dose-response & proximity-to-failure meta-analyses (2023–2024) — Volume drives hypertrophy with diminishing returns; failure isn't required (RIR 1–4); machines ≈ free weights for hypertrophy. Anchors the return-to-training volume ramp and the machine integration.
Wilke (1999) intradiscal pressure; diurnal disc-hydration MRI work — Seated hamstring contraction at 90° hip flexion drives posterior pelvic tilt / lumbar flexion; morning intradiscal pressure runs far higher. Why the leg curl is run prone, and why loaded flexion is kept out of the first hour awake.
